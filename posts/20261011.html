What shape does the sun trace in the sky?

2026-10-11T21:00:00+02:00

A colleague asked me what the shape of the sun's path through the sky over a year is called. I have a master's in astronomy, so this is the kind of thing I should know. The best I could come up with was that it had to be some kind of helix, but I wasn't sure that was the correct mathematical term for the curve. So I asked Claude Code to work it out, and it built a page for it.

The pieces that do have names are the day and the fixed clock time. Over a single day the sun moves along a diurnal circle centred on the celestial pole. Over a year those circles join up into a coil wound around the sky between the two tropics: about 182 turns climbing north, then 182 back down. Earth's tilt drives it up and down. The turns are spread out at the equinoxes and pile up at the solstices, which is literally what "solstice" means: the sun standing still. The elliptical orbit makes the climb uneven, so the northern spring and summer last 186 days and autumn and winter 179.

So is it a helix? Informally, yes, and that's what the page calls it. Strictly, no. In mathematics a helix keeps a constant angle to its axis, like a spring. This curve is wound on a sphere rather than a cylinder, its slant changes through the year, and it turns around at each solstice. There doesn't seem to be a standard name for the whole-year curve. So "helix" wasn't the right term, and there isn't a better one to give my colleague.

The shape people usually mean is a different one. If you look at the sun at the same clock time every day you get the analemma, a figure-8, because the sun runs up to 14 minutes behind the clock in February and 16 minutes ahead in early November. The tilt alone would make a symmetric figure-8 (a curve called the lemniscate of Gerono). The eccentricity is what makes one loop bigger than the other.

The page lets you pick a place and a date, then drag a 3D sky dome around to look at the day's circle, the year's coil and the analemma. There are sliders for "what if Earth had no tilt" or "a more elliptical orbit". Setting the tilt to zero flattens the coil onto the equator; setting the eccentricity to zero makes the analemma symmetric. Below the charts is the full derivation, from Kepler's equation to altitude and azimuth.

The code and the derivation follow standard references (Meeus's Astronomical Algorithms and Smart's Textbook on Spherical Astronomy, among others, listed on the page), and the results were checked against NASA JPL's Horizons ephemeris at 235 dates and four places. The worst disagreement was about 0.01°, roughly a fiftieth of the sun's width, and those comparisons now run as part of the site's tests.
