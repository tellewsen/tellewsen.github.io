The path of the sun across your sky through a day and a year. Each day the sun moves along a circle, and over a year those circles join into a helix that winds up and down between the tropics. If you look at the same clock time every day you get a figure-8, the analemma. The full derivation is below the charts. Drag the dome to turn it.

The mathematics

To get from "Earth orbits the sun" to "the sun is at this altitude and azimuth" takes three rotations, each with its own clock:

  1. The orbit (one year): where the sun is along its yearly track, the ecliptic. The eccentricity makes this motion uneven.
  2. The tilt (fixed): the ecliptic is tilted by ε≈23.44∘\varepsilon \approx 23.44^\circε≈23.44∘ against the celestial equator. This turns the position along the ecliptic into declination and right ascension.
  3. The spin (one day): Earth turns under the sky. Your latitude sets which way your horizon faces on the spinning sphere.

All directions are unit vectors on the celestial sphere: a sphere of radius 1 centred on you. The sun is far enough away that moving around on Earth doesn't change its direction noticeably (parallax ≈ 9″), so a single sphere works for everyone.

§1 Mean motion

Seen from Earth, the sun goes once around the ecliptic per year, in the same sense as Earth goes around the sun. If the orbit were a circle it would move at the constant rate 360∘/365.2596 d≈0.9856∘/d360^\circ / 365.2596\ \text{d} \approx 0.9856^\circ/\text{d}360∘/365.2596 d≈0.9856∘/d. That uniform angle, counted from perihelion (closest approach, around 3 January), is the mean anomaly:

M=357.5291∘+0.98560028∘ nM = 357.5291^\circ + 0.98560028^\circ\, nM=357.5291∘+0.98560028∘n

Here nnn is days since 2000-01-01 12:00 UT (J2000). The direction of perihelion along the ecliptic is ϖ≈282.94∘\varpi \approx 282.94^\circϖ≈282.94∘, measured from the March equinox point. Adding the two gives the mean longitude L=ϖ+ML = \varpi + ML=ϖ+M: where a sun moving at constant speed would be. Mean solar time, and so our clocks, are built on LLL.

§4 The spin, and the equation of time

The hour angle HHH is how far the sky has turned since the sun crossed your meridian (your local north–south line). It is 0 at local noon and positive in the afternoon, and it grows by 15° per hour. Clock time is defined by the uniform sun at LLL. The hour angle of that mean sun at UT hour ttt and east longitude ℓ\ellℓ is

Hmean=15∘(t−12 h)+ℓH_\text{mean} = 15^\circ(t - 12\,\text{h}) + \ellHmean​=15∘(t−12h)+ℓ

Hour angle is measured along the equator, and the mean sun sits at right ascension LLL. The real sun sits at α\alphaα, so its hour angle differs by the angle between the two:

H=Hmean+(L−α)⏟EoT=Hmean+(L−λ)⏟eccentricity+(λ−α)⏟tiltH = H_\text{mean} + \underbrace{(L - \alpha)}_{\text{EoT}} = H_\text{mean} + \underbrace{(L - \lambda)}_{\text{eccentricity}} + \underbrace{(\lambda - \alpha)}_{\text{tilt}}H=Hmean​+EoT(L−α)​​=Hmean​+eccentricity(L−λ)​​+tilt(λ−α)​​

§6 What shape is it?

One day: a circle. Over a day δ\deltaδ is almost constant, so s^⋅e^P=sin⁡δ\hat s \cdot \hat e_P = \sin\deltas^⋅e^P​=sinδ is fixed. The sun stays on the plane perpendicular to the pole axis at height sin⁡δ\sin\deltasinδ. That plane cuts the sphere in a small circle of radius cos⁡δ\cos\deltacosδ, tilted 90∘−φ90^\circ - \varphi90∘−φ from the horizon. Sunrise and sunset are where it crosses altitude h0=−0.833∘h_0 = -0.833^\circh0​=−0.833∘ (refraction plus the sun's radius):

If the right-hand side is below −1 the circle never goes down: the midnight sun, when ∣φ∣>90∘−∣δ∣|\varphi| > 90^\circ - |\delta|∣φ∣>90∘−∣δ∣ on the summer side. If it is above +1 the circle never comes up: polar night.

One year: a helix wound on the sphere. Put the slow and fast motions together. Around the pole axis the angle HHH grows almost uniformly, one turn per day. The height along the axis, sin⁡δ=sin⁡εsin⁡λ(t)\sin\delta = \sin\varepsilon\sin\lambda(t)sinδ=sinεsinλ(t), swings once a year:

Informally this is a helix: a coil wound on the sphere between the two tropics δ=±ε\delta = \pm\varepsilonδ=±ε. Strictly it isn't one. A helix keeps a constant angle to its axis, while this curve's slant changes through the year and reverses at the solstices, and it has no standard name of its own. It winds about 182.6 turns up and 182.6 turns back down. The coil isn't evenly spaced: from §3, the gap between turns is about 0.39° near the equinoxes and shrinks to nothing at the solstices, where the turns pile up. That pile-up is what "solstice" (sol stitium, sun standing still) refers to. The eccentricity makes the climb north take 186.4 days and the return 178.8 (§2). There are 365.2422 turns in a year, so the coil doesn't close. Each year starts about a quarter turn (≈ 6 h) later, and leap days reset it. Without the tilt (ε=0\varepsilon = 0ε=0) the helix flattens onto the celestial equator. Try the tilt slider. The "turns per year" slider spaces out the coil so you can see the spiral.

Same clock time every day: the analemma. Sampling at a fixed clock time removes the fast spin, because HmeanH_\text{mean}Hmean​ is the same at every sample. What is left is the slow wobble:

(H−Hmean, δ)≈( ysin⁡2λ−2esin⁡M,  εsin⁡λ )(H - H_\text{mean},\ \delta) \approx \big(\,y\sin 2\lambda - 2e\sin M,\ \ \varepsilon\sin\lambda\,\big)(H−Hmean​, δ)≈(ysin2λ−2esinM,  εsinλ)

With a circular orbit (e=0e = 0e=0) this is a 2 : 1 Lissajous curve. Write u=sin⁡λu = \sin\lambdau=sinλ: the horizontal coordinate is ysin⁡2λ=2y ucos⁡λ=±2y u1−u2y\sin 2\lambda = 2y\,u\cos\lambda = \pm 2y\,u\sqrt{1-u^2}ysin2λ=2yucosλ=±2yu1−u2​, so x2∝u2(1−u2)x^2 \propto u^2(1-u^2)x2∝u2(1−u2). This is the lemniscate of Gerono, a symmetric figure-8 that crosses itself at the equinoxes. The eccentricity adds a term with a period of one year. Near perihelion (January) both terms push the same way, so the southern (December) lobe grows and the northern (June) lobe shrinks. §5 then projects this onto your sky, tilted by your latitude.

§8 How accurate is it?

These are low-precision formulas. They leave out nutation (Earth's axis nodding with an 18.6-year period), annual aberration (the 20″ shift from Earth's own motion), and the Moon's pull on Earth. They also leave out atmospheric refraction, except in the sunrise and sunset altitude. To see what that costs, the model was compared with NASA JPL's Horizons ephemeris at 235 dates. The comparison covered Oslo, Tromsø, Quito and Sydney once a week through 2026, at different times of day, plus Oslo once a month in 1960 and 2045. The worst disagreements were:

Declination0.009°
Altitude0.011°
Hour angle (equation of time)0.010° = 2.4 s of time
Azimuth0.025° (with the sun 79° up; it grows near the zenith)

That is about a fiftieth of the sun's diameter. These comparisons are part of the site's test suite.
